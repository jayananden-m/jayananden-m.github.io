Testing whether attention helps in platoon control

My MSc dissertation tested whether attention helps a platoon of vehicles follow each other. On the main metric it did not. The reasons why were more useful than a win would have been.

When a line of self-driving vehicles travels close together, braking at the front gets amplified as it moves backward, so the last vehicle brakes harder than the first. If that keeps growing you get a pile-up. This is called string instability and it is the main problem in platoon control.

One idea is to let each vehicle decide which neighbour to pay attention to as conditions change. This is the same mechanism used in language models, and several papers have applied it to platoons and reported improvements.

The problem is that none of them changed only the attention. They also changed the observation scope, or added an action filter, or compared against a baseline that saw less information. The improvements were real, but it was not possible to say how much came from attention and how much came from the other changes.

Changing exactly one thing

So I built four control policies that were identical except for the encoder. The observation, action space, reward, RL algorithm, hyperparameters, training budget and evaluation procedure were all held fixed. Ten learning agents, MAPPO, and SUMO for the simulation.

The four encoders were a plain MLP that passes the observation straight through, a multi-head attention encoder where each follower forms a query from its own state and weights three neighbour slots, and two graph attention networks, which are the most recent approach in the literature.

The attention encoder scored 2.37 m of headway tracking error against 2.48 m for the plain MLP. Across five seeds, with standard deviations of 0.38 and 0.52, that difference is not meaningful. Two held-out scenarios gave the same result.

Why the average was hiding the answer

The aggregate number was a mean over ten vehicles, and the ten vehicles were not behaving the same way.

At the front of the platoon, where a follower has fewer vehicles ahead of it and some neighbour slots are empty, attention was better: 2.47 m against 3.90 m for the baseline. At the rear it was worse. Averaged together the two effects cancelled out.

This makes sense looking back. The attention encoder masks absent neighbours, so an empty slot contributes zero weight instead of being read as a real observation that happens to be zero. Front vehicles are the ones with empty slots, and the baseline has no way to tell the difference.

The mean hid a second difference. On the string stability metric both controllers averaged about the same, but the spread across random seeds was 0.05 for attention and 0.27 for the baseline. Retraining the attention encoder gives you roughly the same controller each time. The baseline varies a lot more.

Testing whether the attention was real

Papers in this area often show an attention heatmap and call it interpretability. A heatmap only shows that the weights exist. It does not show that the policy is using them.

So I broke it on purpose. I took the trained policy, left the weights alone, and corrupted only the attention pathway at evaluation time. Flattening the weights to a uniform average raised the error from 2.4 m to 9.8 m. Forcing all attention onto one neighbour raised it to 31.4 m. Removing the attention context entirely gave 3.3 m.

The last comparison is the useful one. A wrong context is about ten times more damaging than no context at all, which means the policy is reading the attention weights and acting on them rather than just producing them.

The graph attention networks simply didn’t work

Both graph variants failed to converge. Headway error was 114 m and 128 m against roughly 2.4 m for the other two, with survival rates of 0.00 and 0.03 against 1.00. This was not a small loss, it was a failure to learn the task at all.

They also observed more of the platoon than the attention encoder did, which makes the result harder to explain away.

I could probably have fixed this. Their learning rate came from the closest prior work, and raising it to match the other architectures produced partial convergence on some seeds. More tuning would likely have got them there. But tuning one architecture and not the others would have broken the controlled comparison, which was the whole point of the experiment. So I left it and wrote it up as a limitation rather than a general result about graph attention.

What I’d tell myself at the start

My first reward function was all penalties, and under it the best policy was to crash immediately. A failed agent stops accumulating penalty, so failing early scored better than driving well. Adding a capped positive term for speed fixed it. This is worth remembering: the agent optimises what you wrote, not what you meant.

I also reported final-checkpoint results throughout, even though I had better numbers saved from the best checkpoint. Choosing the best checkpoint is a way of tuning on the evaluation metric, so I did not use it.

What I ended up with is a null result on the main metric, a position-dependent effect underneath it, evidence that the attention is doing real work, and a clear failure for the approach the field currently prefers. That is less satisfying than a win, but it is harder to argue with.

All of this is simulation. SUMO's vehicle dynamics are simplified, communication is modelled as instant and lossless, there is no sensor noise, and five seeds with no significance testing is thin. None of it has been tested on a real vehicle.
